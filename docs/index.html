What Predicts Where a Page Ranks?

00 Abstract

We asked whether static content attributes — word count, keyword characteristics, content type, freshness, and generation provider — are associated with a page's average Google Search Console ranking position, well enough to help a content team decide what to review first. We built a reproducible content-grain dataset from FlyRank's pseudonymized warehouse release: 106,063 pages across 39 eligible clients, drawn from a frozen 90-day snapshot window (2026-04-01 to 2026-06-30). On a 100,000-row sample, a regularized linear model (ridge regression) cut mean absolute position error from 11.3 to 9.1 positions against a mean-prediction baseline (R² = 0.32) — and content freshness (creation and update dates) carried more predictive weight than any on-page attribute we measured. The result reads as decision-support, not a ranking formula: content attributes alone explain roughly a third of position variance, so we recommend using them to help prioritize a review queue, not to predict or promise search-ranking outcomes.

01 Introduction

FlyRank publishes content into client websites at scale, then watches search performance and decides what to touch next. With thousands of live pages and limited editorial capacity, the practical question isn't "what does Google's algorithm reward?" — nobody outside Google can answer that from this data — it's narrower and more useful: out of everything already published, which content-side attributes are worth an editor's attention when a page's ranking looks weak?

This is a ranking-signal-analysis project: we are not training a system to place pages in position 1, and we are not claiming to reverse-engineer a search algorithm. We are testing whether attributes that were true about a page before its ranking was observed — its length, its keyword's competitiveness, its age, which team or model produced it — carry enough signal to justify building a prioritized review queue around them. A wrong call here costs editor time on pages that were never going to move, or, worse, teaches a team to over-trust a modest, noisy signal as if it were a lever.

02 Data

The analysis draws on FlyRank's pseudonymized warehouse release (build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703), hosted as gated Parquet on Hugging Face. Three tables were joined: dim_clients (client access profile and history-start dates), dim_content (static content attributes), and fact_content_daily_performance (daily Google Search Console position, aggregated).

The snapshot date was frozen at 2026-06-30 (the latest date present in the warehouse), giving a fixed 90-day window of 2026-04-01 → 2026-06-30 — pinning this window is what makes the dataset re-runnable rather than a rolling target that silently drifts between runs.

Grain: one row per (client_hash_id, content_hash_id). Client and content identifiers are pseudonyms, used only for population filtering and joins — never as model features. No client names, domains, URLs, raw queries, or content titles appear anywhere in the data or in this write-up.

Excluded from features: client_hash_id, content_hash_id (identifiers only), and days_observed (a data-quality signal describing how many days of GSC history back the label — including it as a feature would let the model partly learn from how well-measured a row is, rather than from the content itself).

03 Methodology

Label. gsc_avg_position_90d — the mean of daily GSC average position (clipped to a plausible 1–100 range) over the trailing 90-day window, computed per content item. Lower is better. This is an observed outcome, not a rule someone wrote — the label was never defined from a product decision flag.

Features (17 raw → 19 after engineering). All are static content attributes, knowable independent of the ranking outcome: search_volume, competition, competition_level, cpc, char_count, word_count, main_intent, keyword_char_count, keyword_token_count, url_char_count, backlinks, category_count, content_type, provider_used, model_used, plus two engineered date features, content_age_days and days_since_update, derived from the raw creation/update dates.

Missing values, audited rather than blanket-filled. Every feature's missingness was measured and checked for whether it was informative — whether the label's mean differed meaningfully between rows where the feature was present versus missing.

Every row where the label's mean differed by at least 0.5 positions between "feature present" and "feature missing" was flagged informative — in practice, that was every feature with any missingness at all, so missingness itself carries signal here and was preserved as flags rather than smoothed away.

Validation design. An 80/20 train–test split (80,000 / 20,000 rows, seed 42), with 5-fold cross-validation on the training fold to choose between three candidate models — ridge regression, random forest, and gradient boosting — scored on mean absolute error (MAE). The selected model was then evaluated once, on the held-out test set, against a naive baseline that always predicts the training-set mean position.

Honest caveat on the split: this is a random row-level split, not a client-grouped split. Because a client's pages can appear in both the train and test folds, some of the reported improvement over baseline may reflect the model learning client-level patterns rather than purely content-level ones. See Limitations — a client-holdout re-run is the natural next step, not a nice-to-have.

Two things stand out here, both worth stating carefully rather than dramatically. First, raw backlink count shows essentially zero linear correlation with position (≈0.00007) in this slice — surprising given backlinks' reputation as a classic ranking factor, but backlink counts are almost certainly heavy-tailed and 30.4% of rows have no backlink count at all, so this reads as "not confirmed by a simple correlation check," not "backlinks don't matter." Second, keyword character count shows the strongest simple negative association with position (longer keyword phrases associated with better, lower-numbered positions) — plausible if longer keywords proxy for less competitive, more specific search intent, but this is an association in one snapshot, not a mechanism.

05 Limitations & honest framing

  • Not client-grouped validation. The 80/20 split was random at the row level. Pages from the same client can appear in both folds, so part of the reported error reduction may reflect the model partly memorizing client-level patterns rather than generalizing across genuinely unseen clients. A client-holdout re-run is the direct next step before this number is trusted for a new client.
  • Cross-sectional, not causal. Every feature and the label come from the same 90-day snapshot. Nothing here shows that changing a page's word count, provider, or age would cause its position to move — only that these attributes are associated with position across the pages we observed.
  • Modest explained variance. R² = 0.32 means content attributes alone leave roughly two-thirds of position variance unexplained. This is evidence the content side is one input among several, not evidence the content side is the lever.
  • The label is a proxy, not "search visibility" itself. gsc_avg_position_90d is a 90-day average, clipped to [1, 100] — it smooths over real day-to-day and query-to-query volatility that a single page can show.
  • No algorithm claims. Nothing in this analysis reverse-engineers, infers, or approximates Google's ranking algorithm. All findings are associations observed in one pseudonymized, client-scoped snapshot.
  • Sparse and heavy-tailed fields need care. backlinks and provider_used are missing for 30–58% of rows; the near-zero backlinks correlation should be re-checked on a log scale and against the missingness pattern before treating it as a real finding either way.

06 Ranked recommendations

These are decision-support suggestions for where an editor's limited review time is best spent first — not a guarantee that acting on any one of them moves a page's ranking.

1
strongest signal

Prioritize by content freshness first, not word count

Creation date and update recency carried far more predictive weight than any on-page attribute measured. A review queue ordered by content age / days-since-update is better supported by this data than one ordered by length or keyword metrics.

2
worth investigating

Treat "generation provider unknown" as its own review flag

The missingness of provider_used was itself informative (mean position differed by ~5.6 spots between known and unknown provider) and ranked 7th in importance as a flag alone — pages with unlogged provenance are worth a look, independent of any other signal.

3
needs more data

Don't rule out backlinks yet — recheck on a log scale

The near-zero raw correlation is likely an artifact of a heavy-tailed distribution and 30% missingness, not proof backlinks are irrelevant. Before deprioritizing off-page signals in future lanes, rerun this check with log1p(backlinks) and compare only rows where the field is populated.

4
for the next iteration

Re-validate with a client-holdout split before trusting the MAE gain

Rerun Steps 5–10 of the ML pipeline with a GroupShuffleSplit on client_hash_id and report both numbers side by side. If the gap over baseline shrinks substantially, that's the honest number to publish next.

5
scope check

Use this as a triage signal, not a promise

With R² = 0.32, content attributes should narrow a list of thousands down to a shorter list worth a human look — they should not be presented to a client as an explanation of, or a fix for, a specific page's ranking.

07 Reproducibility

The full pipeline is two notebooks, run in order, both included in this project's repository:

  1. ranking_signal_data_contract.ipynb — connects to the warehouse (hf://datasets/FlyRank/internship-warehouse), applies the population and window filters, validates the contract with explicit assertions, and writes a versioned .csv + machine-readable .manifest.json.
  2. ranking_signal_ml_pipeline.ipynb — re-verifies the contract, runs the missing-value audit, engineers date-based features, splits train/test, cross-validates three candidate models, evaluates the winner against baseline, and saves a versioned model artifact + manifest.

Seeds: RANDOM_STATE = 42 throughout (sampling, train/test split, all three models). Contract version: 1.0.0. Snapshot date: pinned at 2026-06-30 once resolved, so re-runs use the same window rather than a drifting "latest date." Every run also writes a manifest recording row counts, population filters, and the full missing-value decision table, so results can be traced back to the exact parameters that produced them without re-deriving anything from memory.

This analysis is decision-support research produced as part of the FlyRank ML Internship's Applied Search Intelligence track. Claims throughout are observed, measured, or directional — never causal, and never a claim about Google's ranking algorithm.
